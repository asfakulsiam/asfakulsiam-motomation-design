    <h1 id="headline" class="headline">The Last Boatbuilders of Sadarghat</h1>
    <p class="standfirst">On the Buriganga’s south bank, a dozen families still build wooden cargo boats by eye. The river that made them is the thing ending them.</p>

  <section id="keel" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="keel-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">01</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="keel-h" class="title">Keel</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p class="lede">The yard wakes before the river does. At first light on the Buriganga’s south bank the water is the colour of slate, and the far shore is still a band of shadow. Across from the yard, Sadarghat is already loud: launch engines warming, porters calling for work, the first crowds of the day pressing toward the gangways.<a class="fn" href="#note-1" id="ref-1" aria-label="Note 1"><sup>1</sup></a> On this side there is mud, a few tin sheds, and the quiet of people who start work without talking about it.</p>

    <p>A dozen families still build wooden cargo boats here, and they build them by eye. There is no office and no sign. The yard is the bank itself, shared between the families who work this stretch of shore, and the boundary between one family’s boat and the next is the space a man needs to swing an adze.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--wide">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 3 / 2" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 01 <span>·</span> 3:2 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">A keel of sal wood laid on blocks above the mud at first light, the Sadarghat terminal across the water behind it.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>The first thing laid down on any boat in the yard is the keel. This morning it is one long timber of sal,<a class="fn" href="#note-2" id="ref-2" aria-label="Note 2"><sup>2</sup></a> dark and heavy, set on blocks above the mud at the edge of the water. Nobody hurries it. Men walk its length, crouch at one end and sight along it, the way a carpenter checks a plank for twist. They shift a block, sight again, and leave it where it lies.</p>

    <p>A keel is the spine of a boat and the one piece that cannot be wrong. Everything that follows takes its line from this timber: the ribs that rise from it, the planks that close around the ribs, the deck that will one day carry cargo up and down the river. If the keel bends where it should not, the boat carries that bend for the whole of its working life.</p>

    <p>So the first morning of a boat is spent looking. The builders look at the keel from the bank, from the shallows, from the roof of a shed. They look at it with the sun behind them and with the sun in their eyes. When they are satisfied, nobody says so. The work simply moves on.</p>

    <p>The tools in the yard are hand tools. Adzes, saws, chisels and mallets lie on the mud or hang from nails in the sheds. A fire is kept going for the tar. The sounds of the yard are the sounds of those tools: the knock of a mallet, the rasp of a saw, the flat slap of an adze taking a shaving off a timber. Across the river, the sounds are different, and later in this story they will matter.</p>

    <p>The river is part of the workshop. Every finished boat leaves the yard by water. The mud that holds the keel is the river’s own, left behind on the bank. The builders read the river the way they read a timber, for its moods and its warnings, because the river decides when they can work and when they cannot.</p>

    <p>Over four visits between March and June, I watched one boat come up off this mud. Its story is the yard’s story: how a boat is built without a drawing, what it is built from and what that now costs, what is being built across the water instead, who will build the next one, and what happens on the day the river takes it. It begins here, with a timber on the mud and the people looking at it, a long time, before anyone touches it.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="by-eye" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="by-eye-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">02</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="by-eye-h" class="title">By Eye</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>Abdul Karim is sixty-two and the master builder of the yard. There are no drawings for the boats he builds. There is no plan pinned to a shed wall, no set of measurements in a notebook, no template cut from board. There is the keel on the mud, and there is his eye.</p>

    <p>Each rib of a wooden cargo boat is a curve that has to agree with every other curve in the hull. A rib that is a little too full near the keel will push the planks out of line; a rib that is a little too flat will leave a hollow the water finds. In a shipyard that works from drawings, the shape of each rib is worked out on paper first. Here, Karim works it out standing up.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--tall">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 4 / 5" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 02 <span>·</span> 4:5 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">Abdul Karim, 62, sets a rib by sight. Nothing in the yard is drawn first.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>The process looks simple and is not. A rib is roughed out and carried to the keel. Karim has it held in place. He steps back, then steps back again, and looks along the line of the ribs already standing. He walks to the other side of the hull and looks from there. Then he says where the rib should move, and by how much, and the men move it.</p>

    <p>Sometimes the rib comes down and goes back to be cut again. Sometimes it is right the first time. From the outside it is hard to see the difference between the two. To Karim the difference is obvious, and he does not explain it, because the way to understand it is to look as long as he has.</p>

    <figure class="pull">
      <blockquote><p>“I don’t draw a boat. I see it, then I build what I saw.”</p></blockquote>
      <figcaption>Abdul Karim, master builder</figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>What he means becomes clearer as the hull fills out. By the time the last ribs are standing, the boat is already there in outline, a cage of curved timber on the mud. The shape it makes is the shape he saw before the first rib was cut. Nobody else in the yard could have described it in advance. Everyone in the yard can see it now.</p>

    <p>Building by eye is a kind of knowledge that lives in one person at a time. It cannot be photocopied or handed over in a folder. It is passed on the only way it can be: by working next to someone who has it, day after day, until your eye starts to see what theirs sees. That is how an apprentice learns, and it takes years of standing beside the hull.</p>

    <p>It also means the yard depends on people like Karim being there. When he is on the bank, the boat moves forward. When he is not, the men find work that does not need his eye: cutting timber to rough size, tending the fire, cleaning tools. The pace of the whole yard follows the pace of one man walking around a hull and looking.</p>

    <p>There is a pride in this that nobody in the yard puts into words. The boats that come off this mud have no designer’s name on them and no drawing in an archive. They exist because someone saw them first. When a boat built here is working on the river years from now, the curve of its ribs will still be the curve Abdul Karim chose, standing on the mud, one morning in 2026.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="teak-and-tar" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="teak-and-tar-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">03</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="teak-and-tar-h" class="title">Teak and Tar</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>Every boat in the yard is a pile of timber before it is a boat. The keel is sal; the planks and much of the rest come from further away. The timber the builders use now comes from Myanmar, which means that every plank in a hull built on this bank has crossed a border before it reaches the mud.</p>

    <p>The stacks of timber at the top of the yard are the nearest thing it has to a bank account. A family with a good stack can start a boat. A family without one waits. The timber is checked as carefully as the keel: turned over, sighted along, knocked to hear whether it rings true. A plank that looks sound can hide a split, and a split in a hull is a leak waiting for a load.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--wide">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 3 / 2" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 03 <span>·</span> 3:2 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">Timber from Myanmar stacked at the top of the yard. Its price has doubled since 2019.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>That timber has become the hardest part of the work to afford. The price of the wood has doubled since 2019.<a class="fn" href="#note-3" id="ref-3" aria-label="Note 3"><sup>3</sup></a> The boats have not become twice as valuable to the people who buy them, and the families cannot build half a boat. So the difference comes out of everything else: out of the time a boat can wait for the right plank, out of the wages in the yard, out of what is left at the end.</p>

    <p>The planks are worked one by one. Each is shaped to lie against the ribs, eased along the curve, and fixed in place. Where two planks meet, the seam is packed and sealed, and this is where the tar comes in. It is kept warm over the fire in the yard, and its smell hangs over the bank all day, sharp and heavy, until it is part of the air.</p>

    <p>Tarring is slow, messy work, and it cannot be rushed. A seam that is badly sealed will let the river in; on a cargo boat, that means a ruined load or worse. The men who do it work along the hull seam by seam, their hands and forearms black to the elbow, and they go back over the work before anyone calls it done.</p>

    <p>Teak and tar are the two smells of the yard, and both of them come with a price. The tar has to be bought; the fire has to be fed; the timber has to come from Myanmar, at twice what it cost in 2019. Every one of those costs falls on families who set their prices by what a buyer will pay for a wooden boat, and that is a buyer who now has another choice.</p>

    <p>That other choice is visible from the yard. On clear evenings, as the light goes, small blue flashes start up on the far bank, one after another, like a line of matches being struck. The builders do not need to look up to know what they are. They have heard about them for years. Now they can see them every day.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="the-steel-yards" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="the-steel-yards-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">04</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="the-steel-yards-h" class="title">The Steel Yards</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>Across the river, steel launches<a class="fn" href="#note-4" id="ref-4" aria-label="Note 4"><sup>4</sup></a> are being welded. The flashes on the far bank are the welders’ torches, and the hulls that grow under them are made of plate, cut and joined, with no keel of sal and no rib set by eye. They are built faster than a wooden boat, and they cost less. On the south bank, everyone knows both of those facts, and nobody needs them explained.</p>

    <p>From the wooden yard, the steel yards are a view, and the view is hard to look away from. The hulls over there are big and square-edged and grey, with none of the curves that take Karim’s mornings. They go up in a time that the wooden builders measure against their own and do not like. Each new one that slides into the water is a buyer who will not be coming to this side of the river.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--wide">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 3 / 2" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 04 <span>·</span> 3:2 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">Across the Buriganga, a steel launch takes shape under the welders’ torches.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>Nobody in the wooden yard pretends the steel boats are bad boats. They carry cargo. They float. They are cheaper to buy, and they can be had sooner, and for a trader with goods to move those two facts settle most arguments. The wooden builders understand this better than anyone, because they have spent their lives listening to what buyers want.</p>

    <p>What they find hard is the sense that the river itself has changed sides. For as long as anyone in the yard can remember, the river needed wooden boats: to carry cargo, to work the channels, to cross from one bank to the other. The yard existed because the river asked for what it made. One of the builders put it in a sentence, and then asked me not to print his name.</p>

    <figure class="pull pull--right">
      <blockquote><p>“The river used to need us. Now it only needs steel.”</p></blockquote>
      <figcaption>A builder who asked not to be named<a class="fn" href="#note-5" id="ref-5" aria-label="Note 5"><sup>5</sup></a></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>The sentence is less a complaint than a description of the view from the yard: on one side of the water, men with adzes; on the other, men with welding rods; and between them the river, carrying the boats of both.</p>

    <p>The difference between the two banks is also a difference in sound and pace. The wooden yard works at the speed of an eye and a mallet. The steel yards work at the speed of a torch. There is no version of the wooden boat that can be built at the speed of steel, because the thing that makes it a wooden boat built here is the time it takes.</p>

    <p>That leaves the families on the south bank with a choice that is not really theirs to make. They can keep building wooden boats for the buyers who still want them, at a price set partly by the steel yards across the river. Or they can stop. So far, a dozen families have kept going. The river, as it does every year, is about to make the question harder.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="monsoon" class="movement movement--flood grid" aria-labelledby="monsoon-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">05</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="monsoon-h" class="title">Monsoon</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>When the river rises, work stops. There is no meeting about it and no notice. The water comes up the bank, a little more each day, and the yard is a yard only for as long as there is mud to stand on. Then it is the river again.</p>

    <p>The builders have watched this happen all their lives, and they prepare for it the way people prepare for a season, without fuss. Timber is moved up the bank. Tools go into the sheds, then out of the lower sheds and into the higher ones. A boat that is not finished in time is secured where it stands and left to wait for the water to go down.</p>

    <p>The families move too. The houses closest to the river are the first to be reached by the water, and as it climbs the families move up the bank, carrying what they can. They do it every year. The bank that is a workshop in the dry months becomes, for a while, a place where people are waiting for a river to give their work back.</p>

    <p>For a trade that already lives on a narrow margin, the monsoon is a long pause with no income from building. Nothing can be laid on mud that is under water. The timber that was bought at twice its 2019 price sits up the bank and earns nothing. The welders across the river, working in steel, are not stopped in the same way. The gap between the two banks, already wide in the dry months, opens further while the water is high.</p>

    <p>And yet the monsoon is also the reason the yard exists. The water that stops the work is the same water that carries the boats the yard builds. A river that rises and falls with the seasons is a river that needs boats to cross it, to work it and to move goods along it. Everything the builders know, they learned from this river, including when to stop.</p>

    <p>Watching the water come up the bank, it is easy to see the shape of the whole story. The river made this yard: its mud, its timber routes, its need for boats. The river is also where the steel launches go, and where the families retreat from every year. The thing that made the boatbuilders is the thing that is ending them, and both are the same brown water, rising.</p>

    <p>When the river goes down again, it leaves the bank changed. Mud has moved; some of what was secured has shifted. The builders come back down, look at what the water has done, and begin again from the keel. This year, the yard came back down without two of its apprentices.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="apprentices" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="apprentices-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">06</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="apprentices-h" class="title">Apprentices</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>Two apprentices left the yard this year. One stayed. His name is Rubel, he is nineteen, and he is now the youngest person in the yard who is learning to build a boat by eye.</p>

    <p>An apprentice in a wooden boatyard learns by standing next to the work. At first that means carrying: timber from the stacks, tools from the sheds, buckets of tar from the fire. Then it means holding: a rib in place while the master builder looks, a plank against the curve while it is fixed. Only after a long time does it mean deciding.</p>

    <p>Each stage is learned at the hull, in front of the master builder, on boats that real buyers are waiting for, so every mistake costs the yard time it does not have.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--tall">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 4 / 5" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 05 <span>·</span> 4:5 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">Rubel, 19, the apprentice who stayed, at work on the hull beside Abdul Karim.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>The knowledge he is after is the knowledge that lives in Abdul Karim’s eye. There is no shortcut to it. A drawing could be studied at night; a measurement could be written down. Building by eye can only be learned by looking, again and again, at the same kinds of curves, until one day you can see that a rib is wrong before the master says so.</p>

    <p>That is a long apprenticeship to offer a young man when the boats it teaches him to build are losing buyers to the steel yards across the river. The two who left this year made a decision that is easy to understand from the bank. The welding torches on the other side are visible every evening. The years it takes to learn to set a rib are not.</p>

    <p>Rubel stayed. Why he stayed is his to say, and this piece does not put words in his mouth. What can be described is what he does. He is on the bank early. He is at the keel when the ribs go up. When Karim steps back from the hull to look, Rubel steps back too, and looks along the same line.</p>

    <p>There is something in that small movement that sums up the yard. Two men step back from a half-built boat at the same moment, and one of them is learning to see what the other already sees. If the knowledge of building by eye survives on this bank, it will survive because of moments like that, repeated over years, in the space between a master and one apprentice.</p>

    <p>One apprentice is not many. A dozen families build here, and the trade needs hands as well as eyes. But the yard has always depended on the next person being there, and for now, the next person is there. He is nineteen, he is standing next to the keel, and he is watching the boat they are building come close to the day it will meet the river.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="launch" class="movement grid" aria-labelledby="launch-h" tabindex="-1">
    <header class="opener">
      <p class="folio"><span class="folio-n">07</span> <span class="folio-of">of 07</span></p>
      <h2 id="launch-h" class="title">Launch</h2>
      <span class="waterline" aria-hidden="true"></span>
    </header>

    <p>There is no machine in the yard that can move a finished boat. There are people. On the day a boat is launched, forty men come down to the bank to push it into the river.</p>

    <p>The boat sits on the mud where its keel was laid, high and finished, its seams sealed, its timbers dark with tar. The path from where it stands to the water has been prepared: the ground cleared, timbers laid down for the hull to slide over. Everyone in the yard knows the plan, because it is the same plan every time a boat goes in.</p>

    <figure class="plate plate--wide">
      <div class="plate-frame" style="--ratio: 3 / 2" aria-hidden="true"><span class="plate-label">Plate 06 <span>·</span> 3:2 <span>·</span> to come</span></div>
      <figcaption><span class="cap">Forty men push a finished boat down the bank and into the Buriganga.</span> <span class="credit">Photograph: Tanvir Hossain</span></figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>The forty men take their places along the hull. Some are from the yard; some are from the families along the bank; some have come because a launch is something you come to. They set their shoulders and their hands against the timber. Someone calls, and they push together, and the boat does not move. Someone calls again.</p>

    <p>Then it moves. Slowly at first, a hand’s width, then another, the hull grinding over the timbers laid in its path. The calls come faster. The men lean lower, their feet sliding in the mud. The boat that Abdul Karim saw before the first rib was cut slides down the bank toward the water it was built for.</p>

    <p>When it reaches the river, the sound changes. The grinding stops and the hull meets the water, and for a moment the whole yard watches to see how it sits. A boat built by eye is judged at this moment, by the line it makes on the water, the same way its keel was judged, months before, on the mud.</p>

    <p>For the families on the south bank, a launch closes a long account. Every morning spent looking at the keel, every rib set by eye, every plank bought at the new price and every seam sealed with tar is in that hull as it goes into the water. It took patient eyes to judge a keel and forty men to move a boat, and the yard has never had a quicker way to do either.</p>

    <p>The men who pushed it straighten up, wipe their hands on their clothes, and walk back up the bank. Across the river, the welders’ torches are already lit. The new boat rocks on the Buriganga, between the two banks, carrying nothing yet.</p>

    <p>A dozen families still build wooden cargo boats on the south bank, by eye, from timber that costs twice what it did in 2019. Each year, the river rises and stops them, and each year, across the water, the steel yards build more launches, faster and cheaper. The river made this yard and is ending it. For one morning, though, forty men stood in its mud and gave it a boat.</p>
  </section>

  gsap.registerPlugin(ScrollTrigger);
  if (hasSplit) gsap.registerPlugin(SplitText);

  var movements = Array.prototype.slice.call(document.querySelectorAll(".movement"));
  var marks = Array.prototype.slice.call(document.querySelectorAll(".draft-list a"));
  var N = movements.length;

  
  var current = -1;
  function setCurrent(i) {
    if (i === current) return;
    current = i;
    marks.forEach(function (m, k) { if (k === i) m.setAttribute("aria-current", "location"); else m.removeAttribute("aria-current"); });
  }
  movements.forEach(function (sec, i) {
    ScrollTrigger.create({
      trigger: sec, start: "top 55%", end: "bottom 55%",
      onEnter: function () { setCurrent(i); }, onEnterBack: function () { setCurrent(i); },
      onLeave: function () { setCurrent(i); }, onLeaveBack: function () { setCurrent(i - 1); }
    });
  });

  var mm = gsap.matchMedia();
  var start = function () { mm.add({ motion: "(prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)", wide: "(min-width: 1200px)" }, function (ctx) {
    var c = ctx.conditions;
    if (!c.motion) { reveal(); return; }

    
    var lenis = new Lenis({ autoRaf: false, lerp: 0.1, syncTouch: false });
    lenis.on("scroll", ScrollTrigger.update);
    var tick = function (t) { lenis.raf(t * 1000); };
    gsap.ticker.add(tick);
    gsap.ticker.lagSmoothing(0);

    var EXPO = "expo.out", OUT = "power3.out";
    var splits = [];
    function lines(el, opts) {
      if (!hasSplit) return null;
      var s = SplitText.create(el, Object.assign({ type: "lines", mask: "lines", linesClass: "ln" }, opts || {}));
      splits.push(s);
      return s;
    }
    
    function unsplit(s) { return function () { if (s && s.isSplit) s.revert(); }; }

    
    var cover = gsap.timeline({ defaults: { ease: EXPO } });
    cover.from(".masthead-rule", { scaleX: 0, duration: 0.9 }, 0);
    var h1 = lines(".headline");
    if (h1) cover.from(h1.lines, { yPercent: 105, duration: 1.1, stagger: 0.1, onComplete: unsplit(h1) }, 0.15);
    var sf = lines(".standfirst");
    if (sf) cover.from(sf.lines, { yPercent: 102, duration: 0.6, stagger: 0.12, ease: OUT, onComplete: unsplit(sf) }, 0.55);
    cover.from(".kicker, .credits, .draft", { opacity: 0, duration: 0.5, ease: OUT }, 0.9);

    
    movements.forEach(function (sec) {
      var op = sec.querySelector(".opener");
      var title = lines(op.querySelector(".title"));
      var tl = gsap.timeline({ defaults: { ease: EXPO }, scrollTrigger: { trigger: op, start: "top 85%", once: true } });
      tl.from(op.querySelector(".waterline"), { scaleX: 0, duration: 0.8 }, 0)
        .from(op.querySelector(".folio"), { opacity: 0, duration: 0.4, ease: OUT }, 0);
      if (title) tl.from(title.lines, { yPercent: 105, duration: 0.9, stagger: 0.08, onComplete: unsplit(title) }, 0.1);
    });

    
    gsap.utils.toArray(".plate").forEach(function (fig) {
      var frame = fig.querySelector(".plate-frame"), cap = fig.querySelector("figcaption");
      gsap.timeline({ scrollTrigger: { trigger: fig, start: "top 88%", once: true } })
        .from(frame, { clipPath: "inset(100% 0% 0% 0%)", duration: 1.0, ease: EXPO, clearProps: "clipPath" }, 0)
        .from(cap, { opacity: 0, duration: 0.4, ease: OUT }, 0.3);
    });

    
    gsap.utils.toArray(".pull").forEach(function (q) {
      var s = lines(q.querySelector("blockquote p"));
      var tl = gsap.timeline({ scrollTrigger: { trigger: q, start: "top 80%", once: true } });
      if (s) tl.from(s.lines, { yPercent: 100, duration: 0.7, stagger: 0.14, ease: OUT, onComplete: unsplit(s) }, 0);
      tl.from(q.querySelector("figcaption"), { opacity: 0, duration: 0.4, ease: OUT }, ">-0.1");
    });

    
    gsap.timeline({ scrollTrigger: { trigger: ".signoff-mark", start: "top 92%", once: true } })
      .from(".signoff-rule", { scaleX: 0, duration: 0.9, ease: EXPO }, 0)
      .from(".signoff-word", { yPercent: 35, opacity: 0, duration: 1.2, ease: EXPO }, 0.1);

    
    var water = c.wide ? document.querySelector(".draft-water") : document.querySelector(".tidebar-water");
    var prop = c.wide ? "scaleY" : "scaleX";
    var level = gsap.quickTo(water, prop, { duration: 0.4, ease: OUT });
    movements.forEach(function (sec, i) {
      ScrollTrigger.create({
        trigger: sec, start: "top 55%", end: "bottom 55%",
        onUpdate: function (self) { level((i + self.progress) / N); },
        onLeaveBack: function () { if (i === 0) level(0); }
      });
    });

    reveal();
    var refresh = function () { ScrollTrigger.refresh(); };
    if (document.fonts && document.fonts.ready) document.fonts.ready.then(refresh);
    window.addEventListener("load", refresh, { once: true });

    return function () {
      gsap.ticker.remove(tick);
      lenis.destroy();
      splits.forEach(function (s) { s.revert(); });
      gsap.set(water, { clearProps: "transform" });
    };
  }); };

  
  var fontsIn = document.fonts && document.fonts.ready ? document.fonts.ready : Promise.resolve();
  Promise.race([fontsIn, new Promise(function (r) { setTimeout(r, 1200); })]).then(start);
})();
