The Last Boatbuilders of Sadarghat

On the Buriganga’s south bank, a dozen families still build wooden cargo boats by eye. The river that made them is the thing ending them.

01

Part oneKeel

Before the light comes up over the Buriganga, the yard is already awake. It begins with sound rather than sight: a mallet somewhere down the bank, the slap of water against a hull that is not yet a hull, a kettle going on a low fire. The river at this hour is the colour of pewter, and the far bank is only an outline of cranes and roofs. On this side, the south bank, the ground slopes down to the water in a long apron of mud, and on that mud lies a single beam of timber, longer than a room, pale where the adze has opened it.

It is a keel of sal wood,1 and everything that follows will be built from it. The men who laid it down did so the day before, setting it on blocks so that it sits just clear of the wet ground, square to the river. They check it again now, crouching at one end and sighting along its length the way a person might look down the barrel of a pen to see if it is straight. Nobody uses a level. Nobody unrolls a drawing. A boy carries tea along the plank walkway to the men, and the first of them stands up, stretches, and puts his palm flat on the wood as if taking its temperature.

Across the water is Sadarghat,2 the river terminal that gives this whole stretch of bank its name in the mouths of the people who work here. From the yard you can hear it before you can see it properly: horns, engines, a public-address system calling departures. The terminal faces outward, toward the country the river connects. The yard faces the river itself.

A dozen families still build wooden cargo boats along this bank. They are not a company and they are not, exactly, a guild; they are neighbours who share a trade, a shoreline and a habit of lending each other men when a job needs more hands than one household has. Some yards are a single keel with a tarpaulin stretched over a bamboo frame. Others are wider, with a shed for tools and a pile of offcuts that the children turn into toy boats and float in the shallows. The work is passed down rather than taught. Most of the men here were standing on this mud as boys, carrying tea.

What they build is a working boat: broad, shallow, made to carry weight on a river that changes its mind. There is no single plan for it, because there is no paper plan at all. The shape lives in the builders, and it begins with the keel. Get the keel wrong and nothing laid on top of it will sit true. Get it right and the boat, as one of the men put it while we watched the light come up, has already started.

By the time the sun clears the buildings on the far bank, the yard is fully at work. Saws are going. Someone is splitting wood for the fire that will later warm the tar. The keel lies where it was laid, ready for the ribs, and the man who will set them has not yet arrived. Everyone knows when he does. The talk drops. The yard, in the way of yards everywhere, arranges itself around the one person who can see the finished boat before it exists.

On my first visit, in March, I took the keel for a beginning. By the fourth, in June, I understood it as something closer to a statement of faith: that there will be a boat, that someone will want it, and that the river will still be there to receive it when it is done. On this bank, none of those things is any longer certain.

02

Part twoBy Eye

Abdul Karim is sixty-two, slight, and moves around a half-built hull the way a tailor moves around a client: close, unhurried, looking more than touching. He is the master builder in the yard where I spent most of my time, and the other families on the bank defer to him in the way that people defer to someone who has been right for a long time. He does not raise his voice. When he wants a rib moved he points, and when it has been moved enough he lowers his hand.

There are no drawings. I asked about this on every visit, in different ways, half expecting that somewhere in the shed there would be a notebook, a set of measurements, a template cut from board. There is not. Each rib of the boat is set by sight. Karim stands back from the keel, sometimes walking to the water’s edge to see the whole length of it against the river, and decides where the next curve should go and how deep it should be. Then the men cut and fit it, and he looks again.

It is easy to romanticise this, and the builders themselves are wary of anyone who does. Building by eye is not magic; it is memory, built up over a working life, of what a boat looks like when it will carry well and what it looks like when it will not. The eye is checking against thousands of boats that came before. What makes it remarkable is not that it is mysterious but that it is held in so few people, and that it cannot be written down, because the people who hold it have never needed to.

Watching him set ribs is watching a conversation between a man and a shape. A rib goes in. He looks. He walks to the stern and looks along the line of all the ribs together, the way the eye reads a line of handwriting. Sometimes he nods and moves on. Sometimes he says a word, and a rib that looked identical to its neighbours to me comes out and is shaved and goes back in, and when it does even I can see that the line is now smoother. The difference is small. On the river, I was told, it is the difference between a boat that sits level under load and one that does not.

The younger men watch him the way he must once have watched someone else. Nobody explains what they are watching for; it is assumed that if you stand there long enough you will begin to see it. This is how the trade has always moved from one generation to the next on this bank, and it works, as long as there is a next generation standing there.

Karim talks about boats in the present tense even when he is describing ones he built long ago, and he talks about the river as if it were a person with moods. He does not talk much about the future. When I asked him what would happen to the yard, he looked at the keel for a while and then went back to work, which I came to understand was an answer of sorts.

By late morning the boat has the beginning of a body. The ribs rise from the keel like the fingers of two cupped hands, and for the first time you can see what it will be: wide in the middle, rising at the ends, built to sit low and carry. All of it set by eye. None of it written down.

03

Part threeTeak and Tar

A boat built by eye is still built of wood, and the wood has to come from somewhere. On this bank it now comes from Myanmar. The timber arrives as sawn planks and heavy squared lengths, stacked along the top of the slope where the ground is dry, and the builders walk along the stacks the way a cook walks a market, pressing, tapping, sighting down the grain for twists that will fight them later.

The planking is the boat’s skin, and it is where the timber bill is spent. Each plank is bent to the line the ribs have set, fastened, and then fitted against its neighbour so closely that the river cannot find a way in. The work is slow and loud. Planks are steamed or soaked to make them give, and the men lean their weight on them in teams while another drives the fastenings home. The sound carries across the water to the terminal, and on still mornings you can hear the yard from the other bank.

The price of that wood is the first thing anyone on the bank will tell you about, before the steel, before the river, before the apprentices. Timber prices have doubled since 2019.3 The builders do not frame this as a statistic; they frame it as a boat. The same hull, built the same way by the same hands, now costs twice as much in wood as it did before. The customers have not doubled what they are willing to pay.

What the families have done in response is what small trades always do: absorb it. They take on fewer boats at a time and wait for a buyer’s money before ordering the next load of timber. They spend more hours choosing planks so that less is wasted. They reuse offcuts for knees and braces that once would have been cut fresh. Nobody describes any of this as a strategy. It is simply what happens when the margin gets thinner and the work does not get any faster.

Then there is the tar. Once the planking is closed, the seams are caulked and the hull is coated, and for a day or two the yard smells of it: a dark, heavy smell that sits in the throat and gets into clothes. A pot of it is kept warm over a small fire, and the men work it into the seams with a flat tool and then brush it over the planking in long strokes. It is the last thing done before a boat is ready to meet water, and it is the part of the job the children are warned away from.

Tar and timber together are what turn a frame into a vessel. They are also, in the arithmetic of the bank, what turn a boat into a risk. A family that orders wood for a hull is betting money it may not easily get back, on a customer who may decide, while the ribs are still going up, that what he really wants is something faster.

I watched one of the yards coat a finished hull in May. The men worked in silence, mostly, the brushes making a sound like rain on a tin roof. When they were done, the boat looked darker and heavier and somehow older, as if the tar had given it a past. One of the men stood back, wiped his hands on a rag, and looked across the river toward the noise of welding coming from the other side.

04

Part fourThe Steel Yards

From the wooden yards, the steel yards are a light show. Across the river, all day and into the evening, the blue-white flare of welding torches comes and goes against the hulls of steel launches4 under construction. The sound arrives a beat after the light: a crackle, a grinder, the boom of a steel sheet dropped onto a stack. It is the sound of the competition, and on this bank everyone hears it.

The steel launches are faster to build and cheaper to buy. Those two facts are the whole of the case against wood, and the builders do not argue with either of them. A steel hull is assembled from sheets cut to size and welded together; it does not wait for a master to sight along it, and it does not depend on timber from another country. Where a wooden boat is a slow accumulation of decisions, a steel one is a sequence of joins.

The men in the wooden yards speak about the steel yards without much heat. They know some of the welders. A few of them have, at one time or another, crossed the river to do a stretch of work there when their own yard was quiet. They describe steel the way a farmer might describe a dry season: not as an enemy exactly, but as a condition, something that has arrived and is not leaving.

He said it while we were sitting on an upturned crate at the top of the slope, looking across at the flare of the torches. He did not want his name used, and he did not explain why, and I did not press him.5 He said it once and then changed the subject to his children.

What the wooden builders do argue, quietly and among themselves, is what is lost in the trade. A wooden boat can be repaired on any bank by anyone with timber and the right hands. Its shape is the result of someone looking at it. It carries, in the curve of each rib, the judgement of a particular person on a particular morning. None of that shows up in a price. A buyer comparing two boats on the far bank sees one that is ready sooner and costs less, and he is not wrong to choose it.

So the orders drift across the water. Not all at once, and not completely; there are still buyers who want wood, for reasons of habit or loyalty or because a wooden hull suits the work they do. But each season there are a few fewer, and each season the sound from the other side is a little louder. The builders measure the change not in figures but in gaps: a stretch of mud where a keel used to lie, a yard where the tarpaulin has come down and not gone back up.

At dusk the torches are still going across the river. On this side the saws have stopped, and the men are washing at the water’s edge. The light from the welding catches the surface of the Buriganga and breaks into pieces between the two banks, and for a moment it is hard to say which side it belongs to.

05

Part fiveMonsoon

Then the river rises, and everything stops.

The monsoon does not arrive on this bank as weather so much as a change of address. The mud apron where the keels lie, the plank walkways, the fire pits for the tar, the stacks of offcuts — all of it sits on ground that belongs, for part of every year, to the Buriganga. As the rains come and the water climbs, the river takes back the slope one stretch at a time, and the yards retreat ahead of it.

Work stops when the water reaches the keels. There is no question of building on ground that is going under, and no boat can be finished in time to outrun the season if it was not already nearly done. The builders know the signs. They watch the river the way they watch a hull, by eye, and they begin moving things before anyone tells them to: timber first, because timber is money, then tools, then the smaller things that make a yard a place.

The families move up the bank with them. Many of the builders live close to where they work, and the rising river does not distinguish between a yard and a home. Belongings are carried up the slope in bundles and baskets. Children carry what they can. Neighbours who share men during the building season share space during the wet one, crowding into the higher rooms and verandas while the water sits where the work used to be.

I visited during the rains in June, the last of my four visits. The bank I had walked in March was gone, or rather it was river. From the top of the slope you could see the water lapping at the base of a timber stack that had been dragged as high as it would go, and further out, a half-finished hull that had been left too late, roped to a post, its ribs standing up out of the brown water like the bones of something that had lain down to rest.

For a trade that already runs on thin margins, the monsoon is a stretch of months in which there is no building and so no income from building. Some of the men find other work for the season. Some wait. The steel yards across the river, on harder and higher ground, are less troubled by the water; their work goes on under cover, and the flare of the torches continues through the rain.

It is here that the subtitle of this story stops being a figure of speech. The river made these yards: it brought the timber, carried the boats away, gave the builders their customers and their reason. Every year it also takes the yards back for a season, and every year, now, a little more of what it takes does not come back when the water goes down. A family that sits out the monsoon without income may decide, when the bank dries, not to lay another keel.

When the river falls, the mud returns, smoothed and new. The builders come back down the slope, find their posts and their fire pits, and begin again. Most of them. Each year, I was told, it is a little easier to count the ones who do not.

06

Part sixApprentices

A trade that cannot be written down survives only in people, and the people have to stay. This year, in Abdul Karim’s yard, two apprentices left.

Nobody on the bank blames them. The reasons they gave were the reasons anyone would give: the money is uncertain, the work stops for the monsoon, the price of timber keeps rising, and across the river there are yards where a young man can learn to weld and be paid for it sooner. A boy who stands on the mud for years learning to see a boat is making a long bet on a trade whose future the older men themselves will not describe. The two who left made a different bet. It is not a foolish one.

Rubel stayed. He is nineteen, quiet, and moves around the yard with the particular alertness of someone who knows he is being watched and wants to be. He arrives before the master builder and leaves after him. When Karim walks to the water’s edge to sight along a hull, Rubel walks a step behind and to one side, looking where Karim looks.

He does not talk much about why he stayed, and when he does it is in the plain terms of the work. He likes the wood. He likes that a boat is a whole thing you can stand back and look at when it is done. He likes, though he says this more shyly, being trusted with a rib: the first time Karim let him cut and fit one and then looked at it and lowered his hand without a word, which is as close to praise as the yard gets.

What he is learning cannot be hurried, and that is both its value and its problem. There is no course to complete, no certificate at the end. There is only the slow accumulation of looking, the same process that put the shape of a boat into Karim’s eye over a lifetime. Rubel is at the beginning of that process. Whether there will be enough boats left to build for him to reach the end of it is a question nobody in the yard asks out loud.

The older men treat him with a kind of gruff care. They give him the worst jobs, because that is how it is done, and then they stand nearby while he does them. They correct him with a gesture rather than a word. On my visits I saw him hauling planks, stirring tar, holding a rib in place while someone else drove the fastening home, and once, near the end of a long day, standing alone at the end of a half-built hull, sighting along it with one eye closed, the way he had seen it done.

One apprentice is not a future for a trade. But it is not nothing. On a bank where every season seems to subtract something — an order, a family, a stretch of mud where a keel used to lie — Rubel is the one figure in the ledger that has not gone down. The yard seems to know it. When he laughs, which is not often, the older men look up.

07

Part sevenLaunch

A finished wooden boat does not slide into the Buriganga on rails. It is pushed, by hand, by forty men.

Word goes along the bank the day before. The families who lend each other hands through the building season send them now, and men come from the neighbouring yards, from the houses at the top of the slope, from wherever they happen to be. By the morning of the launch there is a crowd on the mud: builders, sons, a few of the welders who have crossed over from the other side, children who have been told to stay back and have not.

The boat sits where its keel was laid, now a whole thing, dark with tar, its ribs hidden behind planking that Karim set by eye one curve at a time. Rollers and greased timbers have been laid in a path down the slope to the water. The men take their places along both sides of the hull, shoulders to the planking, palms flat on the wood the way the first man touched the keel at dawn months before.

Someone calls out a rhythm, and the push begins. It is not one shove but many: a heave, a pause, a heave, the whole crowd leaning in on the call and easing off between, the hull creaking forward a little at a time over the rollers. The sound is extraordinary — a chant rising from forty throats, the groan of timber, the suck of mud — and it carries across the water to the terminal, where people on the decks of waiting vessels turn to look.

Then the bow meets the river. For a moment the boat seems to hesitate, its weight still half on the bank, and the men lean in harder, and then something gives and the hull slides the last stretch and the Buriganga takes it. There is a shout. The boat rocks, settles, and sits level on the water, exactly as Abdul Karim saw it before a single rib was set.

He does not cheer. He walks to the water’s edge, as he did again and again while building it, and looks at it — the whole length of it, against the river. Then he nods. Beside him, a step behind and to one side, Rubel is looking too.

It would be easy to end there, with the boat afloat and the crowd cheering and the apprentice at the master’s shoulder. But the launch is also a departure. The boat belongs to its buyer now, and the stretch of mud where it was built is empty. Across the river the torches are already flaring again. On this bank, the question every launch raises is the same one the keel raised at the start: whether there will be another.

On the morning I left, in June, the water was still high and nobody was building. But at the top of the slope, stacked under a tarpaulin and roped down against the rain, there was a load of timber waiting for the river to fall. Someone, it seemed, still intended to lay a keel.
