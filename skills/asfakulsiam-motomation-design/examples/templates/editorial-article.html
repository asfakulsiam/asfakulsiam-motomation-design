The river took the school. The village rebuilt it on stilts.

By Tahmina Akter · Photographs by Shafiq Alam · 14 min read

In the third week of the monsoon the Kobadak moved forty metres east in a night. By morning the primary school at Ghughudanga was a roof and a flagpole. The headmaster counted the children twice.

What happened next was not an aid project. The parents, most of them shrimp farmers, drew the new school on the back of a calendar: two metres up, bamboo and steel, a boat ramp where the steps had been.

The stilt school, continued

The steel came from a shipbreaking yard in Sitakunda, bought by weight. The bamboo came from the parents' own clumps, cut at the waning moon, when, the farmers say, the sap is low and the borers stay away.

“We didn't build it high so the water couldn't reach it. We built it so the children could watch the water come.”

Mohammad Ali, who chairs the school committee, says the boat ramp was the children's idea. In the flood months it's the only entrance; in the dry months, it's where they eat lunch.
